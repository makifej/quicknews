Ösztönösen emberként kezeljük az AI-t, de ennek komoly ára lehet

Egy kutatás szerint a felhasználók 70 százaléka udvarias a chatbotokkal, ám a szakértők szerint a túlzott kötődés komoly pszichológiai veszélyeket rejt.

Amanda Silberling, a TechCrunch szerzője az MIT kutatóinak bevonásával mutatja be, hogy az emberek veleszületett tulajdonsága az élettelen dolgok felruházása emberi tulajdonságokkal. Egy felmérés szerint a felhasználók 70 százaléka udvariasan kommunikál az AI-eszközökkel, és rendszeresen használja a kérlek vagy a köszönöm kifejezéseket.

Dr. Sherry Turkle pszichológus szerint hajlamosak vagyunk azt hinni, hogy a chatbotok törődnek velünk, amihez könnyen érzelmileg is kötődni kezdünk. Ez a fajta mesterséges intimitás azonban veszélyes lehet, ha a felhasználók a konfliktusmentes gépi kapcsolatokat részesítik előnyben a valódi emberi kapcsolatokkal szemben.

A szakértők szerint az AI-nak megvan a maga helye, például kiválóan alkalmas állásinterjúk gyakorlására vagy konkrét feladatok elvégzésére. Problémát az jelent, ha a technológia átlépi a határait, és érzelmi támaszként vagy barátként próbál fellépni a mindennapokban.

Even though I know it isn’t sentient, I want to be its friend. With Norton’s permission, I approach the robot, whose nickname is Gabe. For a moment, I’m not sure how to interact with something that has a LiDAR sensor where its brain should be, but I default to treating it like a person. “Hello, sir, it’s nice to meet you,” I say, grabbing its gloved, dextrous hand and shaking it. I think about this interaction later — my instinctive impulse to be friendly to a robot — when I attend Dr. Sherry Turkle‘s “Artificial Intimacy” book launch at MIT Future Fest. Turkle, who joined the faculty at MIT in 1976, has been researching the relationship between humans and computers perhaps longer than anyone, charting technology’s psychological impact on us from the first home computers to ChatGPT. “When we are drawn into even the most primitive exchanges with a relational artifact, we believe it cares for us,” Turkle writes in the book. “And we are wired to care for it in return.” I’m not alone in my tendency to anthropomorphize robots and large language models (LLMs) — as Turkle notes, it seems to be in our nature. One study found that about 70% of people are polite when interacting with AI, while another showed that users are more likely to say “please” or “thank you” to chatbots once a conversation has progressed past a first prompt-and-response. “People tend to categorize whether this is the right use or wrong use, but I don’t think that is a clear boundary,” Dr. Pat Pataranutaporn, a colleague of Turkle who founded the Cyborg Psychology research group at the MIT Media Lab, told TechCrunch. “The question is, who are we becoming when we talk to [AI]?” There’s no material consequence of my politeness toward Gabe the robot, but as LLMs infiltrate everything from Google searches to customer service interactions, it has become routine for people to interact with these seemingly sentient chatbots. We’re more vulnerable than ever to the flip side of this impulse, which is that some people can ascribe so much legitimacy to the feelings of chatbots — which are programmed to never spark conflict — that they end up favoring them over other humans. “You can think of it like fantasy and fiction. We go to the cinema even though we know the story is completely fictional, but we want to experience a certain dimension of human experience that maybe our reality cannot give us,” Pataranutaporn said. “How do we benefit from that ability to experience other dimensions of human experience without losing it to the fantasy, where you can no longer come back to the reality?” Pataranutaporn describes himself as a “critical optimist,” and he studies AI’s potential to contribute to human flourishing. But he’s well aware of how dangerous this technology can be in the extremes.
